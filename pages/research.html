The Solidarity Challenge to Epistemic Standpoints
APA Studies in LGBTQ+ Philosophy, Fall 2026.
Abstract
Hannon (2025) argues that deference to marginalized voices cannot ground political solidarity because it fails to generate the moral understanding required for durable collective action. This paper grants that moral understanding can deepen and stabilize movements, but rejects the claim that it must precede solidarity. Drawing on the history of ACT UP! during the AIDS crisis, I develop an alternative picture in which deference functions not as an epistemic failure, but as a practical scaffold—enabling coordination and action under conditions of urgency, disagreement, and partial understanding. ACT UP!’s success, achieved without a unified moral framework or shared theoretical commitments, suggests that solidarity can form through action itself, with moral understanding emerging retrospectively through collective struggle. This praxis-first model challenges the idea that agents must first secure reflective moral agreement before acting. Instead, it demonstrates how deference can catalyze political momentum and generate the very understanding Hannon treats as a prerequisite.

Trans Naming Practices and the Social Meaning of Names
Abstract
Transgender renaming has largely gone unexamined in philosophy of language. I offer an account of it as a replacement speech practice – an attempt to displace the naming practice that holds by default in a community and to install another as the default. Drawing on empirical studies of chosen names, I argue that trans people select names by their social, classificatory, infrastructural, and self-constructive content, and that this content is what the purpose of a chosen name is. Using Goffman’s (1959) notions of line-taking and facework, I then argue that offering a chosen name is an opening gambit whose uptake accumulates across a community rather than occurring at once, and that successful renaming is a markedness reversal. That is, the chosen name becomes the community’s default while the prior name becomes marked and eventually legible as refusal rather than error. Two upshots follow for the recent debate between Camp (2025) and Nowak and Michaelson (2025) over whether the social richness characteristic of nicknames distinguishes them from names people “go-by.” Against Nowak and Michaelson, “go-by” names are a real category, since without one, renaming would have no success condition. Counter to Camp, what makes a name a go-by name is not that its framing effects are incidental, but its standing as a community’s most widely ratified naming practice.

Carving Dynamicness at Its Joints
Abstract
What makes a conversation system dynamic as opposed to static? The classic answer, given by van Benthem (1986, 1996), is that a theory of conversation is static when the system it specifies is eliminative and distributive. Rothschild & Yalcin (2015, 2016) propose an alternative: (strongly) static conversation systems are idempotent and commutative. They present strong staticness as a conservative extension of van Benthem’s criterion, applying to a wider class of systems while agreeing with it wherever his theorem applies. I argue that this is incorrect: their criterion diverges systematically from van Benthem’s within his own domain. Simple Veltman semantics is static by Rothschild & Yalcin’s lights but dynamic by van Benthem’s, and the divergence holds across an entire class of test operators. It arises because van Benthem asks whether an update is intersective using the system’s own worlds, while Rothschild & Yalcin ask only whether some intersective system is isomorphic to the system in question. The generality of their characterization is purchased by the permissiveness of that mechanism.
